The Augmentists are a collection of scientific, political, and philosophical movements united by the belief that humanity is neither complete nor immutable. Rejecting the notion that the human condition should be accepted as it presently exists, Augmentist thinkers advocate the deliberate improvement of mankind through medicine, education, selective breeding, biotechnology, cybernetics, genetic engineering, and other forms of artificial intervention.

Although their methods differ considerably, most Augmentists regard disease, aging, mental limitations, and even human nature itself as problems to be solved rather than permanent features of existence. Critics have frequently accused such movements of reducing human beings to objects of experimentation, while supporters argue that the refusal to improve humanity constitutes a moral failure in its own right.

## 1. Known Factions

### 1.1. Sanitizers

The Sanitizers were among the earliest Augmentist movements to emerge in Brazil. Their origins are generally traced to military physicians, surgeons, and public health officials who served during the Paraguayan War, though many of their intellectual influences can be found in earlier European theories concerning race, heredity, social hygiene, and scientific administration.

Initially concerned with sanitation, disease prevention, and public health, the movement gradually expanded its ambitions beyond medicine. By the late nineteenth century, many Sanitizer thinkers had come to regard poverty, criminality, illness, and social disorder as biological problems requiring scientific management. Humanity itself increasingly became the object of reform.

The movement reached the height of its influence during the early years of the Republic. Several Sanitizer physicians served as advisors to military and civil authorities, viewing large portions of the Brazilian population not as citizens but as subjects for scientific administration. Their proposals frequently combined public health initiatives with increasingly ambitious schemes of social engineering.

Public opposition first became evident during the Vaccine Revolt of 1904, when compulsory health measures, urban reforms, and aggressive state intervention provoked widespread resistance. Although the Sanitizers publicly defended such policies as necessary for national progress, the uprising permanently damaged the movement's reputation among large segments of the population.

Similar tensions emerged during the conflicts of Canudos and Contestado. Internal Sanitizer reports frequently described the inhabitants of both communities not merely as political or religious dissidents but as manifestations of social and biological degeneration. Several expeditions sponsored medical surveys, anthropometric studies, and experimental treatments whose results remain the subject of considerable controversy.

The final blow came with the Spanish Flu pandemic of 1918. Having spent decades promoting the scientific management of society, the Sanitizers found themselves unable to contain one of the deadliest public health disasters in modern history. In the aftermath, many organizations collapsed, while several prominent members became targets of public outrage, political investigations, and, in some accounts, extrajudicial reprisals carried out by military personnel and local authorities.
