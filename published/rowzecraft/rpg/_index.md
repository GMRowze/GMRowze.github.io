I made this system because I think bad systems often rely on te GM having to think "I don't know what happens. I'll make something up."

For me, a good system minimizes ambiguity and "game" decisions, and maximizes creative fictional decisions.

This system is in construction. I will let some ideas public for now while I discuss with friends

## 1. Character Creation

In my opinion, characters are the heart of good RPGs. Thus, I think how they are designed is perhaps the most important part of the system. 

I feel that most RPG systems tend to fall into one of two extremes. On one side, you have the classic attribute spread: Strength, Agility, Intelligence, Perception, Charisma, and so on. These are simple and easy to understand, but they tend to create a few recurring problems:

1. Sometimes it's not obvious which attribute should apply. Is this Intelligence? Observation? Wisdom? Perception?
2. The strongest attributes tend to absorb everything else. In many systems, stacking Charisma or Intelligence is practically a cheat code.
3. They don't have much texture. Two characters with similar numbers often feel the same, and attributes rarely tell you much about who a person actually is.

On the other side, you have systems with enormous skill lists. These solve some of the ambiguity, but create a different problem: half the sheet only matters if the campaign happens to contain the exact situation those skills were written for (I'm looking directly at you, _Electrical Repair_.). Before introducing any stat, I've been running it through four tests:

1. The "could this be another stat?" test: one action should clearly point toward one stat.
2. The "pressure identity" test: each attribute should represent a distinct way people succeed and fail under stress (which then helps players and GM decide which to use).
3. The "operational frequency" test: every attribute should appear constantly during play.
4. The "narrative texture" test: good stats should imply something about the character's identity.

The underlying that I find is that many systems try to represent completely different things using the same mechanics. Natural capability, professional training, personality, education, social background, psychology, and experience all get mixed together. 

So, stealing shamelessly from designers smarter than me (particularly Fallout's separation between attributes and perks, and some of the ideas behind Disco Elysium) I started experimenting with a cleaner separation. 
This eventually led me to split character stats into three different layers:

1. Core Attributes: these stats that represent broad human operating domains under pressure. They represent a more "natural" capability. A "who you are" and "how do you work under pressure".

2. Expertise: these stats represent what the character actually knows and has experience doing. So it's less of a "who you are" and more "what do you know" or "what technical knowled,ge practices, tools, and procedures are familiar to them?"

3. Psychological Layer: this is to give the character flaws and competencies to make him feel alive and interesting.

In a short while I'll describe which ones I have selected.

I think this makes a good distinction both in 
A military veteran and a career criminal might both be highly disciplined, observant, and resilient. Their attributes could look very similar. Their expertise, however, places them in completely different worlds. Military expertise gives access to military institutions, planning methods, culture, and ways of thinking while criminality gives access to black markets, covert practices, criminal networks, and illicit opportunities.

This is also why I try to avoid making expertises too profession-oriented. Once expertises become things like "Lockpicking", "Forgery", "Smuggling", and "Theft", players start treating them like a CV. "Criminality" is usually more interesting than all four of those skills separately because it describes an entire ecosystem of practice rather than a collection of isolated actions.

Thus, when rolling, the players and GM can ask themselves:

1. What kind of pressure is acting on the character?
2. What kind of familiarity or expertise applies here?

For example, repairing a damaged generator during a flood might involve Engineering as the relevant expertise, with another roll drawing on Focus, Ingenuity, Coordination, or Resolve.

Interrogating a traumatized witness might involve Journalism, Criminality, or Bureaucracy as relevant expertises, while the pressure side of the roll relies on Empathy, Composure, or Rhetoric.

And just to make it extra clear, I will just make the distinction more obvious between some that may be confused:

* Awareness vs Agility (Awareness notices danger. Agility responds to it. If a beam falls from the ceiling, Awareness notices it, Agility determines whether you dodge it.)

* Insight vs Empathy (Insight understands situations, motives, inconsistencies, and hidden patterns. Empathy understands emotional states Insight notices somebody is hiding information. Empathy realizes they are ashamed rather than hostile.)

* Focus vs Discipline vs Composure (Focus: maintaining attention. Discipline: maintaining self-control. Composure: maintaining emotional stability.) Following tracks through chaos uses Focus. Not attacking a suspect uses Discipline. Not panicking after finding a corpse uses Composure. Instinct covers rapid subconscious judgments under uncertainty. Good uses include sensing ambush risk, hesitating before entering a dangerous room, or prioritizing threats during a crisis. It should not replace observation or deduction. "I instinctively know the cultist is lying" is not an Instinct check.

* Knowledge, Focus, and Memory (When memory is involved: Knowledge remembers facts, Focus remembers situational details, Empathy remembers emotional and social details)

As mentioned, these attributes may change.

### 1.3. Psychological Aspects

The reason Lovecraft-inspired games are gripping is, in my view, not fundamentally the monsters or the unknown itself. They are gripping because they are dramas about imperfect people operating under pressure, uncertainty, discovery, and consequences. Characters uncover information that matters (often emotionally) to them, make decisions with incomplete understanding, and then deal with the resulting escalation and fallout. This creates emergent situations where the most emotionally believable decision is not always the safest or most rational one. Thus, the core gameplay loop becomes: (i) move/discover, (ii) decide, (iii) suffer the consequences.

For now, there are two useful phrases to ponder:
- "The only thing worth writing about is the human heart in conflict with itself" (William Faulkner). That is, good scenarios place the character’s values, fears, loyalties, and desires into conflict.
- "It is absurd to divide people into good and bad. People are either charming or tedious" (Oscar Wilde). In other words, interesting characters are not defined by their attributes or their morality, but by contradictions, motives, flaws, beliefs, and pressures.

Thus, I believe that the best characters are competent but flawed, the best sessions are when decisions reveal character beliefs (or challenge them), and the consequences change characters. 

Thus, the final layer of character creation is psychological. Attributes describe how a person functions under pressure. Expertise describes the worlds and practices they understand. The psychological layer answers a different question entirely: what psychologically anchors or destabilizes this person?

The first component is the character's anchors. These are the people, beliefs, loyalties, and values that provide emotional stability and reinforce persistence under pressure. An anchor might be faith, family, duty, patriotism, scientific truth, professional pride, comradeship, love, revenge, or an ideology. Whatever form they take, anchors answer a simple question: what keeps this person moving when things become difficult?

The second component is trauma. These are the experiences, fears, wounds, and unresolved conflicts that destabilize a character under specific forms of pressure. Trauma may manifest as war memories, abandonment, guilt, claustrophobia, addiction, grief, betrayal, a crisis of faith, or fear of authority. Where anchors provide stability, traumas create vulnerabilities. They answer the opposite question: what breaks this person?

A third category is a separate mechanic: drives. Not every psychologically significant trait is a source of stability or a source of weakness. Some are engines. Ambition, vengeance, curiosity, martyrdom, ideological fanaticism, and the need for recognition can all push a character forward while simultaneously creating new problems. A powerful drive may strengthen Resolve while encouraging recklessness, damaging relationships, or escalating conflicts that would otherwise have been avoided.

What interests me about this approach is that it gives the GM meaningful ways to pressure a character beyond simple physical danger. The game can challenge beliefs, loyalties, fears, identities, and emotional wounds in the same way that it challenges physical capabilities. A veteran's trauma is not simply flavor text; it becomes something that can be activated by explosions, confinement, helplessness, or chaotic battlefield conditions. Likewise, an anchor such as "my younger sister" becomes relevant during moments of protection, fear of loss, family conflict, or despair. This feels far more psychologically believable than systems that simply reward players for roleplaying.
