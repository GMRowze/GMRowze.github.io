Hi! Hello, my name is G. M. Rowze, and I am a writer (when I am not procrastinating, of course). I mostly write fiction, especially historical fiction, though I tend to mix genres quite freely mystery, speculative fiction, post-apocalypse, alternate history, and whatever else manages to feel right at the time of writing. This is the first post on the blog and, in a way, the inauguration of the Rowze Archive itself.

I am not particularly fond of talking about myself. I usually find author biographies somewhat drab, self-important, and derivative. Moreover, at the time of writing, my only published works (outside STEM) are mostly short essays on culture, history, and folklore. My only biggest, finished and public work was a limited internal publication of my first novel, Latlander, at the École Centrale de Lille. As such, I assume most readers arriving here will probably already know me personally and need little introduction.

If this is your first contact with my work, I would actually recommend skipping this post altogether and reading one of the stories first.

As the title suggests, this entry is META, and here I will explain what the Archive is, who exactly "G.M. Rowze" (and the archivist) is supposed to be, and how this "in-universe" literature is structured.

First, you must understand that I have severe difficulty *not thinking on stories* and *not creating stories*. My mind is constantly producing new plots, characters, settings, fragments of dialogue, historical divergences, and strange little details that may never become books at all ([This is also why it is often difficult for me to focus on or finish a single work](adhd.md). Some of my closest readers may remember that the 2022 draft of *The Latlander* exceeded a thousand pages due to the sheer number of characters, subplots, and historical tangents involved.)

But this also means that I am constantly thinking about how characters and worlds interact beyond the immediate plot itself. In my opinion, the weakest fictional universes are the ones that only exist while the reader is looking directly at them. Once the plot moves elsewhere, everything freezes. Cities stop breathing, politics stop evolving, and characters become suspended props waiting for the protagonist to arrive again.

In contrast, the strongest fictional worlds feel alive independently of the story being told. There is no real “trick” to this. It happens because certain writers become so immersed in their creations that the world continues moving naturally around the narrative. The obvious examples here are people like George R. R. Martin and J. R. R. Tolkien.

As a result, whenever I create a setting, character, conflict, or historical event, I immediately begin thinking about the wider consequences around it. There are the large questions, of course (*Who governs the city? What happened twenty years before the story? What rumors do ordinary people believe? Which wars, migrations, religions, or economic pressures shaped the setting before the protagonist was ever born?*), but also the small ones (*Which books do the characters read in their downtime?*).

For example, I spent more time than I should judging whether Detective Martinsons liked the Sherlock Holmes novels or not.

This (involuntary) process of thinking tends to lead me to surprising new ideas for the stories and characters. I have always liked Martin's "gardener" analogy for writing. Rather than designing a rigid blueprint where every detail exists only to serve a predefined structure, I often find stories evolving organically as new ideas emerge. Entire characters, themes, or historical developments sometimes appear halfway through the writing process and permanently alter the direction of a book. To quote him directly:

> "My stories grow and evolve and change as I write them.  I generally know where I am going, sure… the final destinations, the big set pieces, they have been my head for years… for decades (...) There are lots of devils in the details, though, and sometimes the ground changes under my feet as the words pour forth. (...) Things are growing… and changing, as does happen with us gardeners.   Things twist, things change, new ideas come to me (thank you, muse), old ideas prove unworkable, I write, I rewrite, I restructure, I rip everything apart and rewrite again, I go through doors that lead nowhere, and doors that open on marvels." <br/> — [George R. R. Martin in "A Winter Garden" blog post, July 2022](https://georgerrmartin.com/notablog/2022/07/08/a-winter-garden/)

So, whenever I write a story, I tend to imagine the surrounding world continuing to exist independently of the immediate plot As my mind invents settings, conflicts, and characters, it also starts "connecting the dots" and make everything gradually gain internal weight and begin affecting other parts of the world around them. One small idea generates another, which generates another, until the setting develops what one friend once described as "the complexity of feeling alive" without requiring artificial complexity for its own sake. 

One other thing that quickly becomes apparent about me is that I am deeply fascinated by historiography, archives, literary transmission, and the strange ways culture preserves memory over time. Tolkien famously created languages because he loved philology so much that the stories emerged naturally from them. I, on the other hand, am an engineer and researcher by formation, and I tend to obsess over archives, disputed texts, incomplete manuscripts, conflicting interpretations, literary chronology, and the way knowledge itself becomes organized and transmitted across generations (people age, misremember events, disagree on facts, reinterpret old stories).

(It probably says something worrying about me that one of my favorite books is Aristotle’s slightly insane attempt to categorize nearly everything in existence through the [*Organon*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organon).)

So in the end, after years of writing, more writing, and rewriting (and very rarely publishing), I gradually ended up creating what I can only really describe as the ["Rowze-verse"](rowzeverse.md). It is the shared-universe and timeline where all the stories, characters, and historical events exist. For example, characters from [*Detectives of the Empire*](detectives.md) coexist in the same world as figures from [*Latlander*](latlander.md), even if they never directly meet on the page.

But this is where things become slightly stranger (and, in my opinion, way more interesting). Inside this universe, there also exists a fictional G. M. Rowze. This fictional version of me is not exactly the author of the stories themselves, but rather an editor, translator, archivist, critic, and publisher who lived between the early twentieth century and the 1970s. He existed *inside* the world where these events supposedly took place and became responsible for publishing the texts that you (the reader) can read.

For example, Gendarme Edgars from *Detectives of the Empire* is, within the internal logic of the setting, treated as a real historical individual. His letters and memoirs were (in-universe) preserved by his family before eventually reaching (in-universe) Rowze, who later edited and republished them in Paris during the 1930s. This means that other characters living in the same universe may themselves have read these books, debated their contents, criticized their translations, or interacted with Rowze directly.

As a result, the Rowzeverse has books inside its books. Readers exist inside the world. Critics, translators, historians, rival interpretations, disputed editions, incomplete manuscripts, censored publications, and literary controversies also exist inside the world. This creates a second fictional layer nested within the first one, which means the final product you (the reader) can read has itself a publicaiton history of many authors, editors, translators, critics behind it.

The term *diegetic* becomes useful here. In literary theory, diegetic elements are things that exist and are experienced *inside* the world of the story itself, while non-diegetic elements exist only for the audience outside it. So when I refer to "Fictional Rowze," (or any other term like "Publisher Rowze" or "Diegetic Rowze"). I am referring to the fictional G. M. Rowze who exists *inside* the universe itself. He is a fully lived-in historical character with his own political opinions, friendships, literary tastes, correspondences, intellectual evolution, and personal flaws.

Intentionally, I managed do make this neat trick where his shortened biography is similar to my own real one (Latvian-Brazilian, studies in France, military service in Latvia, participates in literary circles), and so forth. This allows me to blur the boundary between author, editor, translator, and character in a way that I personally find both amusing and intellectually interesting.

The people inside the setting who supposedly authored the original texts later collected by Rowze are referred to as the "sub-authors". These include memoirists, letter writers, historians, diarists, journalists, military officers, folklorists, and fictional novelists whose works were later edited, translated, preserved, or republished by Rowze himself. Edgars is the best example here. He "authored" his own letters, which were later organized and published by Rowze.

Yes, I know this already sounds mildly insane, but bear with me for a moment.

This sort of literary layering is not entirely new. Tolkien presented The Lord of the Rings as a translated historical text derived from the "Red Book of Westmarch". That is, Tolkien (supposedly) is just this scholar that found this old book and translated it. Equally, Borges frequently invented fictional books, authors, citations, and academic disputes inside his stories. Pessoa fragmented himself into entire literary personalities with distinct biographies and writing styles. In all these cases, the boundary between real-life author and fictional author becomes intentionally blurred.

But of course, this Fictional Rowze is not simply "me inside the story." He is _almost_ me but not quite. He has his own ideas, political opinions, flaws, literary tastes, friendships, correspondences, and historical limitations. He is a man shaped by his own century, and not every belief or action of his is necessarily something I personally endorse. I think this separation is important because it allows him to exist as an actual historical personality within the setting rather than merely as a mouthpiece for me (the real-life author).

But this metafictional "in-universe" structure would feel shallow if it stopped there. Writers die every day, and the debates surrounding their legacy often become just as interesting as the works themselves. This is where "The Archive" and "The Archivist" enter the picture.

Within the internal chronology of the Rowze-verse, Rowze eventually dies and leaves behind a large personal library, correspondence collection, and literary archive that later becomes associated with the municipal cultural system of Curitiba. Decades afterward, a distant relative ("The Archivist") begins digitizing, organizing, annotating, and publishing these materials online through the very website you are reading now. And yes: the Archivist is also fictional. _Almost_ me but not quite.

Like Fictional Rowze, the Archivist overlaps heavily with my own real biography. He is also a computer engineer, researcher, archivist, and obsessive collector of obscure documents. He also has his own personality, preferences, flaws, and bad habits (as an example of bad habits, this includes the Archivist occasionally using the Archive blog to discuss completely unrelated topics like video games. He has no decency to separate work and personal matters!).

So, within the internal logic of the setting, the Archivist "digitizing" texts and publishing them online becomes the in-universe mechanism through which I publish stories, essays, notes, and documents while remaining inside the fictional continuity itself. And you, the reader, also becomes an in-universe character reading it.

When I created the Archive, it became a way of organizing the fictional universe from within the fictional universe itself while simultaneously functioning as the real-world platform through which I publish these stories. I am, in this regard, very much an ["If you wish to make an apple pie from scratch, you must first invent the universe."](Rowzeisms.md) type of writer. At some point, I apparently ended up creating the fictional Wikipedia of my own setting simply because the setting itself demanded a place to preserve its books, archives, arguments, correspondences, and histories.

Of course, every once in a while I need to drop this veneer/theater of in-universe and have to speak directly to you (the reader). This post is one of those occasions. (It would make very little sense for the Archivist himself to write something like this because, from his perspective, he genuinely inhabits that world and those books were already published decades ago.) For this reason, posts (and wiki entries) that intentionally step outside the fictional continuity will always carry the `[META]` tag and will be authored by `"Meta-Rowze."`

(One final distinction worth mentioning involves writing style itself. Publisher Rowze writes with a much more early-to-mid twentieth century literary voice, while the Archivist naturally writes more like a contemporary internet-era STEM person. As Meta-Rowze, I will generally adopts the latter style as well.)


So, in simplified terms:

- (Fictional) G.M. Rowze is an in-universe historical figure.
- The Archivist is a later in-universe figure responsible for preserving and digitizing Rowze's legacy.
- The sub-authors are the fictional individuals who supposedly authored the original texts later collected by Rowze.
- (Real) G.M. Rowze is me, the real-life author. Meta-Rowze is the out-of-universe tag I use whenever I need to address the reader directly outside the fictional framework.
- All of them overlap with me to different degrees, but none of them are fully identical to me.
