Paris, 1933

Dear A. Farias,

I received your manuscript last Thursday and have now read it thrice: twice as a reader (which is the more honest method), and once as a publisher (which is the less innocent one). You requested my help as a friend in discussing some early ideas, and in that you shall indeed have a good friend in me. The pages have life, and your prologue in particular sets a tone unlike what I expected.

I will not say your ambition will be an easy one. Few Brazilian authors have attempted fantasy and politics as you have, and both are difficult materials for the general public. I will reserve my more particular remarks for another letter, but in this one I shall discuss something that came to me as I reviewed the manuscript.

You see, I believe you and I, as writers (though I consider myself more an editor and publisher than a writer), have a talent for generating lived-in stories. But because these stories have life, they also have the dangers of life. They wander (which is not, in itself, negative; I am suspicious of books that never wander). Still, there is a difference between the road that bends because the land requires it, and the road that bends because the surveyor has lost his instruments. Or, in other words, because the writer has become lost in the world he himself created.

You told me in your last letter that stories do not come to you in order. You called yourself a “gardener-type” of writer, one whose stories grow naturally and who adapts as he goes along. This I believe (I would go further and say that stories seldom come in order to anyone worth reading). A mechanical imagination receives a plot as an engineer receives an floor plan, while a living imagination receives fragments and reconstructs the world as a whole: an opening, a fortress in a failing empire, a rumour heard and spread (again, I commend your prologue for it). Only later does the author discover whether these pieces belong to the same house.

There is no shame in this. Indeed, as I told you, it may be the better method. The novelist who knows too much too early often produces characters who behave irrationally or coldly in order to go where the plot requires them to go. These “architect novelists” often end up with characters who arrive on the scene when needed, say and do what is required for the plot, and then retire when the chapter has no further use for them. The result is orderly and dead.

But the opposite danger is no less real. Authors like us, refusing all architecture out of reverence for life, may end with mud instead of earth. One cannot grow a garden by throwing seeds at a field and calling the confusion natural. Gardens require beds, paths, stakes, pruning, and a certain cruelty exercised in the service of flourishing.

What I now propose to you is something I have proposed for myself. I often start stories by thinking of a few key scenes, characters, and themes. Then I often return to the beginning and give the thing a first attempt, if only to taste the work. Usually this is the easy part. It is often in chapter three or four that we must stop the garden from turning into a swamp. This is where my method comes in.

When you find yourself losing the force of the beginning, return to the general outline, and plan your chapters into arcs. When I say “plan,” do not read it as deciding in advance every gesture, glance, and revelation. That way lies a novel full of obedient corpses.

The error of many young authors is to think that chapters are containers for events, but they are not. A chapter is not a box in which the author places happenings until the box is full. A chapter is an operation performed upon the reader’s understanding. I would therefore ask of each chapter several questions.

First: what happens?

Second: why does this chapter exist in the architecture of the whole? Does it open a door, close a door, move suspicion, change allegiance, sharpen danger, expose hypocrisy, or force the protagonist into a new relation with himself?

Third: what does the principal character want here? (Note that this is not what the author wants, nor what you feel the theme requires.) What does the person in the scene want, even if he misunderstands his own desire?

Fourth: what resists him? This resistance need not always be a villain. It may be law, custom, memory, hunger, love, shame, weather, God, class, money, or the simple fact that another person is also alive, with plans and desires of his own, and therefore not a device.

Fifth: how does this chapter stand in relation to the themes and thematic questions of the book?

It is this fifth question that most concerns me. Chapters do not always know whether they are stating the book’s problem, demonstrating it, contradicting it, or forcing the character to choose under it. Permit me to arrange this more plainly in the table below:

| Level | Name           | What the chapter does                               | Weak version                  | Strong version                                                                 |
| ----: | -------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
|     1 | Atmosphere     | Shows the world where the theme exists              | “This is a violent place.”    | The setting itself teaches why people trust, fear, obey, resist, or desire something. |
|     2 | Statement      | A character expresses a belief                      | “Power is necessary.”         | The protagonist and the opposing forces articulate incompatible moral systems. |
|     3 | Demonstration  | The plot proves a belief plausible                  | The protagonist defeats someone bad. | A belief genuinely works, and the reader sees why the character trusts it. |
|     4 | Contradiction  | The chapter shows the cost or limit of that belief  | “That belief is bad too.”     | The character acts according to a plausible belief, but the result exposes its moral cost. |
|     5 | Choice         | The character must act between two incomplete goods | “Do it or don’t do it.”       | The character must choose between two goods, each carrying guilt, loss, or compromise. |
|     6 | Transformation | The character’s worldview changes irreversibly      | The protagonist feels sad.    | The character can no longer believe the old belief in the same clean or innocent way. |

At the lowest level, a chapter may create atmosphere. It teaches the reader what kind of world this is. Such chapters are not decorative when properly made. A frontier town, a Paris office, a priest’s room, a railway carriage, a factory yard, or a widow’s kitchen may each teach the reader what the characters believe before any character has spoken doctrine. The world itself has opinions, and sometimes it speaks before the people do.

At the next level, a chapter may state a belief. A man says that law is the only thing standing between civilization and the wolf. A woman says that mercy is not weakness. A clerk says that one must obey orders because the alternative is chaos. A revolutionary says that blood is regrettable but historically necessary. Such statements are useful, provided they are not allowed to remain isolated speeches.

Then comes demonstration. The plot proves a belief plausible. If a man says that force is necessary, let him be right once. Let lawful force save a child, stop a murderer, or prevent a village from being devoured by its own fear. A weak writer only refutes the beliefs he dislikes. A stronger writer allows them their hour of victory.

After demonstration must come contradiction. The same belief that saved life must now demand a cost. The lawful act must leave a wound not recorded in the law. The merciful refusal must expose the innocent to danger. The priest’s prudence must resemble cowardice, or the soldier’s courage must resemble vanity. The chapter must not merely say, “This too is complicated.” It must make the complication visible in consequence.

Above that is choice. Here the character must act between two incomplete goods, or between one good contaminated by necessity and another good made dangerous by purity. It is not enough to choose between good and evil. Such choices have their place, but they seldom carry the full weight of a serious book. Fiction becomes serious when a man must choose between justice and mercy, truth and loyalty, peace and protection, law and conscience. It is in such choices that character and theme cease to be separate matters.

Finally, at the highest level, there is transformation. A chapter of this kind does not merely change the situation. It changes the instrument by which the character understands situations. After such a chapter, the man cannot return to his former innocence, even if all external facts appear as before. He may still carry the same revolver, sign the same warrant, pray the same prayer, sleep beside the same wife, or walk the same street. But the thing has changed in his hand.

You will notice that I am not asking every chapter to reach the last height. That would be intolerable. I once read that a book composed entirely of revelations is like a church composed entirely of bells (it would deafen everyone and shelter no one). But the author should know where each chapter stands on the ladder. The theme of a story is not merely a sequence of events. It is the change in meaning that each event imposes upon the events before it.

Finally, you may also ask yourself this question: in which layer of the story does the central conflict truly live?

| Layer         | Planning question                                         |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| World         | What kind of society produces this conflict?              |
| Institution   | What does law, church, family, class, or state claim to protect? |
| Character     | What does the protagonist believe is necessary?           |
| Counter-world | Who lives by a rival moral logic?                         |
| Pressure      | What event makes the existing answers insufficient?       |
| Choice        | What must the protagonist do when no option is clean?     |
| Residue       | What remains unresolved after the plot is solved?         |

If you cannot answer these questions, the book may still have battles, but it does not yet have a true conflict. A true conflict is not merely what prevents the protagonist from obtaining what he wants but rather exposes the insufficiency of the world by forcing every available answer to reveal its cost.

This is related to what critics sometimes call the argument of a book (though critics usually discover the argument after finishing the book and then speak as if they wrote the themes themselves). I mean something simpler and more practical. Every serious story asks a question that cannot be answered honestly at once.

You should attempt something similar in your own manuscript. I do not mean that you should limit yourself to this method (God spare us from authors who learn only the form of a story and copy it, thinking they are being deep). I mean that you should ask what belief your protagonist carries at the beginning, what rival belief he encounters, what event makes both beliefs inadequate, and what residue remains when the plot has spent itself.

The word residue is important. I distrust endings that tidy the soul too neatly. In your story, the mystery may be solved and the villain may be exposed. But if the book has asked a serious question, something should remain unsettled in the reader, not because the author has failed to answer, but because the answer itself has weight and must be carried.

Take a few sheets of paper. For each chapter, write:

- What is the chapter’s function?
- What visibly happens?
- What does the protagonist want?
- What does the opposing force want?
- What belief about the world is being tested?
- At what level of pressure does the chapter operate: atmosphere, statement, demonstration, contradiction, choice, or transformation?
- What is different by the end?
- What question carries the reader forward?

Do not answer elegantly, as elegance at this stage is an enemy. Write like a carpenter marking beams. If the chapter cannot survive such coarse handling, it probably had no foundation.

You may find, when doing this, that some chapters exist only because you enjoyed writing them. As an author, I do not believe this to be a crime, for enjoyment is not evidence of uselessness. But as an editor, I must say each chapter must earn its place. It must either carry plot, deepen character, sharpen the governing question, or alter the reader’s relation to what came before. If it does none of these things, preserve the good paragraph and bury the chapter without ceremony.

I would also advise you to distinguish between the chapter’s external event and its moral event. These are often confused.

The external event is what the summary can contain: a man arrives, a letter is found, a gun is fired, a woman vanishes, a witness lies, a house burns.

The moral event is what no summary can report honestly. On this same story: a man discovers he enjoys being necessary, a woman realizes her mercy has become pride, a coward successfully disguises himself as prudent, a magistrate finds that the law he serves has borrowed its authority from fear.

There is one further matter. The taxonomy must not become a machine for delivering messages. It should not eliminate discovery, nor should it leave no room for the character to surprise you. Its purpose is not to make the book clever (never, ever believe in authors or editors who try to make "clever" books), but to help you as a creator to answer to your own themes.

This is the reason I prefer this method to the ordinary outline. An outline says: first this happens, then this happens, then this happens. A moral outline says: first the character believes this, then the world seems to confirm it, then the same belief wounds someone, then an opposing belief appears noble, then that opposing belief fails under pressure, then the character must choose and act.

This is, in the end, the distinction between a novel with a theme and a sermon. A sermon knows what it thinks before it begins. A novel earns the right to think and tell its thesis by suffering through its own contradictions.

Finally, as is my advice with every "writing tip" is that we cannot substitute a good story with tricks. Not every book requires such "cleverness", and not every story improves by being distilled by its themes. A tale must first be worth hearing, and your tale is worth hearing already, it only needs trimming and constraint to discuss its own ideas. On that, a reader may forgive a difficult idea but will not forgive boredom merely because it has been decorated with intelligence.

Therefore use this taxonomy if it helps the chapters breathe (as it helped me). If it causes them to march like soldiers, throw it away and return to the living page.

Yours,

G. M. Rowze
