Curitiba, 1948

Dear Hermanis V.,

I have at last seen the picture you recommended to me, The Angel and the Badman. You must forgive my delay; the theatres of Curitiba conduct their diplomacy with Hollywood at a somewhat leisurely pace. Here, the American West (and its movies) arrives as if it had come by boat. Still, perhaps the delay was useful. A film of this sort is better seen after the noise of its advertisement has passed.

You were right to recommend it, and I greatly enjoyed the picture.

You were also right to call it ["simple, yet not simplistic"](../../misc/rowzeisms) in its approach. The theme is presented almost as a parable. The characters are clear, and the moral lines are equally clean, yet the picture is not shallow. Rather, its simplicity allows the question of violence to stand visibly on its own.

The question is not whether violence is ugly (every child knows that, though men spend whole literary generations pretending they have discovered it). The question is whether violence can be used for peace or self-defense, or (as Tolstoi would have it) violence itself for whatever good reason will always be a contradiction. Thus, the film asks if violence becomes clean when placed in the hands of a better man.

That, in my view, is why the film interested me beyond its surface pleasures. Of course, the picture excels in recreating the myth of the American West: there are bandits, horses, guns, a girl of uncommon innocence, and the vast country which Americans seem to require whenever they wish to argue about the of their country soul. It's easy to dissect all the plot points: a strange and violent man comes into town wounded. He is received and restored by peaceful people, and is made to discover that mercy is not weakness. Yet he must also reckon with the old question: whether such peaceful people can survive in a world where other men men use their peaceful nature as a tool to exploit then.

The cinematography is simple, but effective. I shall never grow tired of seeing the vast open country of the West, which I had fallen in love with years before I ever had the chance to see it. And while you know my reservations concerning Mr. Wayne, he was not without merit here. 

The film’s finest instinct is to avoid making peace look merely passive. The Quaker household is not made of cowards, and their gentleness/meekness is not the result of ignorance. It is a choice, and therefore much stronger than the ordinary sentimentality that passes for virtue in certain other stories. Many lesser authors make the peaceful side too eager a victim, and thus reduce pacifism as a moral choice. There is a great difference between the man who does not strike because he cannot, and the man who does not strike because he has judged the blow and refused it. The first is helpless. The second is terrifying, and much more interesting. As the old adage goes, a good man is not the man incapable of doing evil, he is the man capable of evil who chooses not to do so.

You also asked me (or perhaps only hinted, which is the safer method when approaching an editor’s vanity) whether I thought of Edgars while watching it. Naturally I did. I could hardly avoid it. When we edited A Revolver for the Czar's Frontier for publication all those years ago, I believed I was helping bring to light an account of law at the edge of empire. The themes on Edgar's letters and its relation to the movie are clear: if violence can be used to protect the peaceful.

But unlike the film’s protagonist, Edgars was never a "bad man" in the manner of Mr. Wayne’s character. He was a dutiful, lawful, restrained, brave man who did not love violence. Yet, while he held no love for violence, his flaw was that he accepted it (which is often the more dangerous thing). The cruel man may at least know that he is cruel, but the lawful man is tempted by another illusion: that because his violence is authorized, it has ceased to be violence.

His story is not about a bad gunslinger finding redemption in peace, but about a lawful man discovering that lawful violence is still violence. In this sense, Edgars starts his narrative with firm in the belief that the gun is regrettable but necessary. In his mind, the revolver is not a symbol of domination. It is a tool of civilization against lawlessness.

We must not be under the impression that we, as a society, dislike violence or see no reason to it. Violence is horrible, but perhaps necessary. A frontier without force or law does not become paradise, rather, it becomes a pantry for predators. And this is what the American West and the Urals have in common: the pacifist is not allowed the cheap luxury of pretending that evil is imaginary, but neither is the man of law allowed the opposite luxury of pretending that evil can be mastered without entering him.

It is this lack of groundness that makes other stories pacifism so often fail. They make peace beautiful by making evil stupid. They arrange for the wolf to be shamed by the lamb. But wolves are rarely stupid or ashamed. Peace in this stories is easy and violence avoidable, which is rarely the case in real life. 

Perhaps this is why our age, having seen so much machinery of death, still dreams of the clean weapon in the clean hand. We do not really believe in peace. We believe in the final, righteous application of force after which peace will no longer need to be defended. It is an old pagan hope, dressed now in uniforms, committees, revolutions, and police reports (and is the wrongly placed faith that, in the last war, I found both in the eyes of my fellow soldiers and in the eyes of the Germans). Thus, I think Edgars’ message is more relevant than ever.

If he were simply violent, the pacifist family could teach him kindness, but because he is lawful, his stay in the Urals asks whether justice must always ultimately be backed by force. If law requires violence to survive, what kind of goodness can law actually preserve? Can a good man remain good when his goodness depends on sanctioned force? We are accustomed to asking whether a shot was justified (it is a legal question, and a necessary one). But the soul asks a second question after: what did the justification do to the man who used it? Did it humble him, or did it secretly enthrone him? Did he return from violence poorer, or did he discover that he liked the authority of necessity? 

Thus, we see him and the world around him justify violence by saying, "I am allowed to use force because I serve law." In the end, Edgars comes closer to the harder truth of "the law may permit force, but permission does not cleanse the soul of what force does." A story of peace earns its theology only when it permits evil to remain genuinely dangerous, peace to remain costly, and goodness to be made with doubts. The problem is not solved by saying "never use force," any more than it is solved by saying "use force only for good." Both sentences are too small for the tragedy of human government. 

In both accounts (Edgars’ and the film’s) [providence](../../misc/rowzeisms) is the climax. The protagonists refuse to resort to violence, knowing full well that they cannot outgun evil, and that even if they could, they might do so only at the cost of the beliefs of the people they protected. In refusing violence, they allow good to prevail through Providence. Note here that I make a clear distinction between "not fighting" and "not resorting to violence." 

These considerations, I believe, are what made both our minds remember Edgars while watching the film. Both accounts are concerned with violence, refusal, and the movement of the weapon from the hand into the conscience. The American story, in my opinion, is cleaner and resolves the matter more sweetly. But the argument is related. To adapt Edgars’ iconic phrase: a revolver can defend a house. It cannot teach a man what a house is for.

Finally, the picture made me think of theological matters (which I know you care little about, but which must still be said). The majority of Christians, I find, are blind to this discussion altogether. Most of them assume peace to be the condition after victory. In reality, for us, it is a command given before victory, and sometimes without any promise of victory at all. This does not abolish law, nor does not abolish the the soldier or the policeman, but it prevents them from becoming priests of their own necessity (priests of "the greater good"). A man may be required to bear the sword, yet he must not worship it because he was required to bear it.

That is the whole matter, or as much of it as I can put in one letter without becoming intolerable. You were right about the picture. Its plot is simple, but simplicity is not a defect when it leaves room for judgment.

Finally, congratulations on finishing your most recent studies.

Yours,

G. M. Rowze
