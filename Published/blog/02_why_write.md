There once was a time, dear reader, when stories were not primarily products packaged neatly into little stacks of paper that needed to be purchased in order to be consumed. Not as physical copies printed and distributed by licensed intermediaries (which you are utterly forbidden from copying and printing yourself!), nor in the even stranger modern form of digital files that disappear the moment a subscription expires[^4]. 

No, stories were these strange things people told each other because human beings seem almost biologically incapable of *not* telling stories. Around fires, in taverns, in temples, during voyages, during work, during war, before sleep, in Tumblr blogs, around the office coffee machine. People invented characters, retold old tales or inside jokes, exaggerated or mixed histories together, borrowed fragments from others, forgot endings or invented new ones, and passed stories forward simply because doing so was deeply human. Storytelling, much like singing, dancing, joking, drawing, or whistling while working, is simply one of those deeply human things people naturally do because they enjoy doing them.

And most importantly: these stories often had no singular owner. They belonged to the people. As in, the *folk*.

And that concept is massively important to how you should approach my work.

In those pre-cultural industry era, there were no corporations claiming perpetual control over characters and how they were allowed to be portrayed. There was no centralized canon department deciding which retelling was “official,” and no intellectual property disputes over whether someone had the right to reinterpret, reuse, continue, or reimagine a story or character.

I could spend hours discussing my deep love for folk traditions and oral literature. I could write pages about how extraordinary it is that one of the oldest surviving literary works in human history, the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, already begins by remembering the “ancient days,” as though even its own world was looking backward into a deeper remembered past. I could talk about the possibility that some myths surrounding the “Seven Sisters” constellation may preserve fragments of observations tens of thousands of years old, from a time when the stars appeared more visibly grouped in the night sky. Or how some historians and anthropologists now speculate that certain fae and changeling traditions may partially reflect historical social perceptions surrounding autism.

And of course, there is the endlessly fascinating realization that stories once dismissed as pure fantasy later turned out to preserve genuine historical memory beneath layers of myth, exaggeration, and poetic invention. The *Iliad* and the city of Troy itself are probably the most famous examples. I mean, how insane is it that a (supposedly) 8th century B.C. blind poet named Homer managed to preserve, with surprising accuracy, fragments of Bronze Age geography, political memory, and even elements of chariot warfare from the 12th century B.C.[^1]? Likewise, there are scholars who argue that the myth of the Minotaur may partially reflect cultural memories surrounding the decline and conquest of the Minoan civilization.


I will not dwell too much on this discussion because far greater thinkers than me have already tackled it extensively. In short, we (as modern people) have this view that myths are regarded as "mere primitive beliefs", that oral stories are "primitive traditions" (and unreliable!). It suffices to say that the social role of folk stories was bonding. They helped societies preserve memory, construct shared identities, discuss morality, explore sacrifice, confront death, explain the unknown, and imagine futures that did not yet exist. Long before modern mass media, fiction already functioned as a cultural conversation spanning generations. Tolkien argued that myths and fairy stories were not childish distortions of truth, but one of the ways human beings approached truth itself (an idea that eventually became his concept of the "True Myth")[^2]. 

What I want to tackle instead is our modern obsession with authorship itself and the relation between the author and his "fans", and why my entire writing philosophy goes against it. But, let us construct this argument in parts. First, I want to explain why I believe people will continue writing even in the age of AI-generated literature. Then I want to discuss our modern myth of the "lone literary genius," humanity's obsession with singular authorship, and why fanfiction is probably much closer to the natural historical state of storytelling than modern intellectual property law would like to admit. To then finally top if off with what is my personal writing and why you should ["get on the train"](Rowzeisms.md) too.

# Capitalism, Slop, and Why AI Does Not Particularly Bother Me

First, I need to dismiss the simplistic idea that all of this is merely the fault of capitalism. Again, smarter people than me (in this case, the Frankfurt School) have already defined and criticized the "cultural industry" extensively. Industrial mass media absolutely encourages the production of passive, standardized entertainment optimized for consumption rather than participation. In modern society, stories increasingly become “content”: disposable, passively consumed, algorithmically distributed, and endlessly metrified.

To the modern corporate executive, stories are products and therefore intellectual properties to be managed, monetized, protected, and distributed through whatever platform currently governs human attention spans. And because these average Western corporate men increasingly experience art through trend forecasting, market analytics, consultant presentations, executive committees, engagement metrics, and quarterly growth expectations, you end up with this bizarre flattening of culture. This is not to say there are no talented people working on these productions (Of course there are!). The problem is that the entire industrial framework surrounding modern entertainment increasingly rewards predictability and broad marketability.

Which is why, even back in the 2010s, we were already living in the era of slop (ahem, *Marvel*). Nothing can become *too* strange, too uncomfortable, too specific, too eccentric, too tragic, too sincere, or too difficult because the product first needs to survive committee review while remaining legible across four international demographics and seventeen market segments. Which is how you eventually arrive at that peculiar modern phenomenon where you can watch a trailer and more or less reconstruct the emotional structure of the entire film before it even releases.

And to be clear: this is not because audiences are stupid. Quite the opposite. Audiences recognize patterns very quickly. The problem is that industrial entertainment has become deeply afraid of genuine risk because genuine risk threatens profitability. So yes, human beings invented *slop* long before the first generative AI models were ever released.

Which is why I do not particularly care for the debate over whether AI-generated art is "real art." Human beings have always been perfectly capable of producing tasteless, derivative, gauche, kitsch, tacky, cliché-ridden work. AI did not invent slop, the only thing it truly changed was the *scale* at which it can be generated. And quite frankly, in our current year, it is still easy to identify which works were made by flawed human authors whose passions, obsessions, limitations, eccentricities, talents, and errors remain visible on the page over the strange flattened aesthetic produced by the big corporate committee-think machines.

Moreover, there seems to be a great the existential fear and anxiety by some writers in regards to generative AI. In their minds, AI threatens to  "outcompete" them, flooding the market with infinite optimized prose capable of satisfying audience expectations more efficiently than any human being can. And perhaps they will, afterall, slop sells (ahem, _Marvel again_)

But I think this fear often reveals a deeper misunderstanding about why people write in the first place. If the primary motivation for writing is fame, prestige, market success, or professional recognition inside an industrial content economy, then yes: AI is genuinely threatening. Perhaps it is just me, but I am far too blue-collar and working-class to let those *petite-bourgeoise* anxieties stop me. Storytelling existed long before modern publishing markets and will continue existing long after our current cultural and economic paradigms eventually collapse with the _big oops_[^3]. (And yes, I do mean a *paradigm*. Our assumption that people are supposed to earn money by telling stories is itself historically contingent. There was once a time in human history, perhaps when we were still hunter-gatherers, when not a single human being gained anything from storytelling.)

Well, anything material, I mean. As I already said at the beginning: human beings tell stories because human beings *like* telling stories. People continue singing despite recorded music existing. They continue painting despite cameras existing. They continue telling jokes despite having already heard thousands before. Storytelling is not merely industrial production. It is a deeply human participatory behavior. So AI or no AI, I would still write. These stories existed in my imagination long before any model. I write because I want them to exist outside myself. Without audience, prestige or money, I would still write.

So, before moving forward: capitalism did not invent slop. What it did was massively incentivize the flattening and standardization of culture for the sake of broad marketability and scalable consumption, while simultaneously creating the modern legal and economic machinery surrounding intellectual property.

# The Myth of the Lone Genius 

Before we talk about the legal aspects of ownership, we first need to talk about what I see as a much older and more fundamental human tendency of seeking to attach ideas, discoveries, and creations to singular exceptional individuals. We see this both in science (with the myth of the "lone geniuses" and in history, with the "great man history" paradigm)

Somewhere in the past few centuries (perhaps around the Enlightenment, though that is merely a personal suspicion rather than a rigorously sourced historical claim), Western society became increasingly obsessed with the figure of the rational individual genius. The great inventor/brilliant philosopher/singular artist producing supposedly original works almost _ex nihilo_ through the sheer force of intellect and creativity, and this deeply affects our understanding of authorship.

Again, many people blame capitalism alone for our modern obsession with ownership, authorship, and creative competitiveness but I do not think that is entirely correct. Capitalism certainly industrialized and monetized these tendencies, but I suspect the psychological roots are much older and far more human.

Human beings seem almost instinctively drawn toward "great man" narratives and reduce messy collective historical processes into singular narratives of heroic figures because it is easier understand. Of course, this tendency creates a lot of unhealthy outlooks. From people being obsessed with not sharing their work or sharing credit, to people refusing to accept that their work does not exist in a vacumm and is as important as the people interactig with it.

For writers, I think George Lucas is probably one of the clearest modern examples of this phenomenon. At some point in the 1990's, a misconception grew that the idea that *Star Wars* "belonged" exclusively to George Lucas, despite the fact that the franchise itself was always shaped by enormous collaborative efforts involving editors, actors, composers, concept artists, special effects teams, writers, costume designers, fan communities, expanded universe authors, video game developers, and decades of reinterpretation by audiences themselves. Then, when George Lucas created the Original Trillogy, no one even dared question him.

And to be clear: this is not me attacking Lucas specifically. Quite the opposite. I think the cult surrounding singular authorship is often deeply unfair to the authors themselves. To quote John Donne and Newton: *no man is an island* and *if I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants*. Therefore, no creator emerges in isolation from culture. Every writer absorbs language, symbols, artistic traditions, folk memory, political anxieties, aesthetics, jokes, philosophies, and storytelling structures inherited from thousands of other human beings across generations. Even the most "original" creator on Earth is still participating in an enormous ongoing cultural conversation that existed long before them and will continue long after they die.

Which is another reason why I think many fears surrounding AI-generated literature are somewhat overstated. Sure, some works are there to merely serve as momentary entertainment entertainment. But most stories also also fulfill social, emotional, cultural, and existential roles, and they help people connect with one another and construct shared references and values. Again, storytelling belongs to a habit of having a shared identity and belonging to a community.

Which is also why I increasingly find absurd the modern tendency to treat certain creators as though they exist completely above or outside the communities that gave meaning to their work in the first place. And yes, I do think authors possess *some* degree of moral responsibility toward their readers and communities (looking at you, _George Martin_). Not in the childish sense that fans are entitled to unlimited content on demand, nor that artists should become servants of audience expectation, but in the much simpler sense that literature itself is relational. 

Which is also why I find it strange when certain readers treat creators as entirely beyond criticism, or when people insist that a single "correct" interpretation of a work must permanently exist simply because the original author declared it so. Art does not function that way. Every art work is a conversation between creator, audience, context, and culture itself.

I would never have become a writer had other people not first told me stories. I would not write if my family and community had not filled my childhood with tales and stories (no matter how small or personal). Likewise, many of the stories I now write only exist because hundreds of other authors before me planted seeds of plots and settings that later grew into something else entirely. In other words: I am only a writer because other people once shared stories with me, and my stories only fully become stories once another person reads them.

Which is also why the Rowzeverse was intentionally designed as this strange *pot-pourri* of fictional people constantly interacting with stories, texts, translations, archives, interpretations, and literary debates. Because stories do not exist in isolation.

# The Problem of Intellectual Property (and the Blessing of Fanfics)

As I already mentioned in the introduction, for most of human history intellectual creation simply did not function this way. Songs, folk tales, legends, and even many written stories circulated socially long before anyone imagined fictional worlds existing as legally protected corporate territories. In fact, the vast majority of stories we still possess today only survived because people (the *folk*) kept adapting, modifying, and retelling them across generations.

But somewhere along the way, we created this idea that cultural creations are the property of those who created them. 
And to be fair, this was not entirely bad. At least initially, intellectual property protections allowed artists and creators to avoid starving while watching others freely appropriate their work. Creators could finally receive rightfully recognition and compensation for what they produced.

Well, *recognition* and *compensation* are doing some very heavy lifting here, and we will return to that discussion later. But the problem for now is that, once you establish that a person can own a story, it becomes very easy to justify a corporation owning one too (and the justification is very easy: if the corporation paid your salary in an agreed contract, it has the right to take an ownership share of your creation, right?). I am not some radical arguing intellectual property should be completely abolished, nor that creators deserve no ownership whatsoever over their work. But I do think modern society increasingly treats stories less like cultural expressions and more like permanently ownable assets. And historically speaking, this is still extremely unusual.

The idea that fictional worlds should exist indefinitely as well-defined legal properties controlled by centralized ownership structures is a very recent development in the broader timeline of human culture (Personally, I also doubt it will remain stable forever, especially once generative AI starts making the very concept of creative exclusivity increasingly difficult to enforce in practice. But that is a much larger discussion for another time). What matters for our conversation here is that we have increasingly raised generations of people who instinctively believe meaningful participation in storytelling requires permission. Moreover, the practical consequence of this is that if someone owns the culture, you are not a participant of it anymore. Little by little you are no longer a reader but a "consumer" (or at best, a "fan"): someone to be retained, segmented, monetized, and fed increasingly frictionless content products.

Which is one of the reasons I find fanfiction so philosophically fascinating. Because beneath all the internet stereotypes, questionable prose, and occasionally catastrophic writing decisions, fanfiction reflects something extremely old and deeply human: the desire not merely to consume stories, but to inhabit them a little. To continue thinking about them after the book ends. To reinterpret characters, alter outcomes, argue with themes, imagine different paths, or simply spend more time inside a world that meant something to you. Truth be told, I think fanfiction is probably one of the closest modern equivalents we have to the old folk process of collectively inhabiting stories as something shared rather than consumed.

This is also one of the reasons I structured the entire Rowzeverse as this strange byzantine-like literary ecosystem composed of texts written by many different in-universe authors, later collected, edited, translated, annotated, and republished by G. M. Rowze himself. Importantly, even in-universe Rowze does not entirely "own" these stories. His role is often much closer to that of an archivist, editor, translator, folklorist, or literary curator than that of a singular omnipotent author. Many of the texts supposedly existed before he ever touched them. Others circulated informally for years before later becoming systematized editions under his name. He himself took this very _laissez-faire_ approach to his publications, and this was very intentional on my part.

In a certain sense, I wanted the Rowzeverse itself to preserve this older folk-like relationship with storytelling, where stories belong less to isolated creators and more to the people inhabiting the culture around them. Which is why, even inside the fictional universe itself, there are debates surrounding authorship, ownership, preservation, translation, adaptation, and the social role of literature. A work like *Detectives of the Empire* is not treated as some perfectly sealed intellectual property object. The letters of Edgars, for example, supposedly circulated loosely during the Civil War long before Rowze later collected, organized, translated, and republished them into more definitive editions. 

So even internally, the Rowzeverse treats stories less as fixed products and more as living cultural artifacts constantly being transmitted, interpreted, argued over, and belonging to many authors across generations.

And bear with me for a moment here, because this is where things become slightly metafictional again. The characters you encounter while reading my stories are not exactly the same characters I originally imagined while writing them. They cannot be. EVER. Every reader inevitably reconstructs them slightly differently through their own memories, experiences, assumptions, emotions, biases, and imagination. Whenever I describe a city, a face, a voice, or a moment, each reader silently fills the gaps in their own particular way.

In other words, the story only fully exists inside the reader's mind. Every single reader is creating inside their head a slightly different story.

Which also means that stories do not really exist in isolation on paper. A fictional universe that nobody reads, imagines, discusses, reinterprets, argues about, misremembers, quotes, or emotionally inhabits is, in a certain sense, only partially alive. And this is ultimately why I arrived at the more radical conclusion that every reader is, to some degree, a participant in the storytelling process of the Rowzeverse itself. The Archive was intentionally designed to leave room for this older folk-like relationship with stories: one where readers are not merely passive consumers standing outside the work, but as people can choose to engage with it "in-universe". Whenever a real-life reader reinterprets or discusses, they are doing the equivalent of what Rowze or the the Archivist are doing.

Now, to be clear, I do not mean this in the sense that every reader literally becomes a canonical character running around inside the setting like some kind of self-insert fanfiction protagonist. (At least, not necessarily, but that is a much stranger conversation for another day. But no, the archive is NOT collaborative-fiction like the SCP foundation) What I mean is something simpler: readers become part of the wider conversation surrounding the stories.

If the Archivist exists in-universe as someone cataloguing, preserving, discussing, and interacting with these works in the modern day, then the reader can also be understood as another person participating in that same living literary world surrounding the stories. That is, my friends, _getting on the train_.

But I will end things here before this turns into an even longer metafictional rant than it already has.
